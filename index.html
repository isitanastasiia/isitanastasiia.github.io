I

Room I · Winter landscape

The Path Home

Oil on canvas · 2026

A winter road at the hour when snow stops being white. The whole picture runs on two temperatures — the rose of a low sun and the blue of everything it can’t reach — plus one small warm thing: a house.

S-curve of the path · the trunk’s vertical · the line of the snowfield · thirds

  1. The road isn’t white — it’s lilac, rose and pale blue. Snow is a mirror for the sky, so painting it “white” would be a lie. The S-curve starts at the bottom edge, right where you stand, and pulls you in toward the house.

  2. The smallest object and the warmest dark on the canvas, sitting low on the left third line, half-buried. Its tiny size is exactly what makes the spruces feel enormous.

  3. One hard vertical cutting the picture top to bottom, right in front of us. Painters call this a repoussoir: a near object that pushes everything else back and opens up the depth.

  4. Rose melting into grey, blended wet-into-wet with no visible strokes — the calmest surface in the painting. The sun is low and hidden behind the trees: late dusk, or very early morning.

  5. Light paint dragged over dark with an almost dry brush. The bristles break the stroke apart, and that broken edge reads as snow caught on needles. Light lands only on the tops.

  6. Quick upward flicks in ochre and grey — the only warm texture in the foreground. Their rhythm repeats the tall trunks, just smaller.

II

Room II · Seascape

The Rock Holds

Oil on canvas · 2026

A rock lit from the side, standing in a sea that refuses to be one colour. The painting is an argument between yellow and violet, and the surf is where the argument gets loudest.

the rock’s triangle · a high horizon · stones leading in on the diagonal

  1. Built, not drawn. The stone is made of short, square, flat strokes laid side by side like bricks, each one a small plane catching light. Cézanne built his mountains the same way.

  2. The sunlit face is yellow and ochre; the shadow side isn’t brown, it’s violet — yellow’s complementary colour. That’s the impressionist rule of shadows, and it’s why the rock seems to glow.

  3. A tiny tree leaning out, its foliage dotted in red, ochre and green. It gives the scale — and the story: something alive holding on to something hard.

  4. Thick white flicks thrown against the darkest base of the rock. Contact is drama: the brightest whites in the picture sit right next to the deepest darks, exactly where water hits stone.

  5. Never just “blue”. Horizontal dashes of cobalt, cerulean, lilac and mint sit side by side and mix in your eye instead of on the palette. Broken colour — Monet’s way.

  6. Pink and peach scumbled in crossing directions. Not a single cloud is drawn; the light itself is the subject.

  7. Duller, browner, lower in contrast. They anchor the bottom edge and step diagonally up toward the rock, leading the eye in.

III

Room III · Portrait (of a sort)

Not Amused

Oil on canvas · 2026

A cat in a clown wig, deeply unimpressed. It’s funny for about one second — then the blue stripes start reading as tears. Absurd on purpose, the way she loves contemporary art: a joke that makes you look twice.

the wig’s great oval · the nose’s axis · the eye line · red calls to red

  1. Red, yellow, green and a touch of blue, laid on thick in round, twisting dabs — the texture literally is the curl. It’s the loudest place on the canvas and it owns the whole top third.

  2. Blue vertical stripes under the eyes. Both. This is the old sad-clown idea — Watteau’s Pierrot, Picasso’s saltimbanques — painted onto a cat.

  3. One red circle and one downturned line. The red repeats the wig and ties the face to it; the drooping mouth carries the entire mood.

  4. Black almonds with a single white highlight each, glancing sideways rather than at us. The cat is somewhere else, untouched by our laughter.

  5. Not flat white: the strokes follow the direction of the fur, with greys and cool blues tucked under the highlights, so the face stays round.

  6. Heavy grey strokes on the chest read like a clown’s collar — a Pierrot ruff made of fur.

  7. Flat khaki, almost no texture: a quiet field that lets the wig scream. The cat is pushed to the left, leaving empty air beside it.

In her own words

I’m in love!!! With art in general, honestly. I’m a big fan of classical art as well, but I appreciate pretty much every period. I love seeing how people expressed themselves in different eras, and how art reflects the way they saw and understood the world around them.

I’m particularly into impressionism and post-impressionism, especially artists like Berthe Morisot, Odilon Redon and Monet. I also love modern and contemporary art, especially Rothko and Yves Klein. What fascinates me about contemporary art is how something seemingly absurd or unconventional can make you question things and see them from a completely different perspective.

I’m also really interested in performance art. For example, Marina Abramović and Ulay’s walk along the Great Wall of China is something I find incredibly meaningful. I love how art can go beyond paintings and sculptures and become an experience, an idea — or even a way of expressing something that words can’t quite capture.
